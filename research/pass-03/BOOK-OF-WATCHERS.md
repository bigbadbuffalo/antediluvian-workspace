## 1. Witness controls

The Book of the Watchers is an Aramaic Second Temple composition, generally placed by the third or early second century BCE, with uncertain dates for constituent material. The early copies set limits on circulation, distinct from the narrative's primeval date. For cross-language verse numbering this dossier uses the [OCP edition and data](https://pseudepigrapha.org/reader.html?doc=1En.xml) (SRC-024; [edition XML](https://pseudepigrapha.org/static/docs/1En.xml)). Its Greek pane draws on Charles and Black and its Geʿez pane principally on John Rylands Ethiopic MS 23, an eighteenth-century copy (SRC-036). Charles's English, when cited, is SRC-020. OCP contains editorial and translation layers, including a correction layer for the Ethiopic and modern Aramaic supplements. Here “trace” means the editor prints surviving letters or partially legible letters in that location. An entire verse, a proposed line restoration and a visible letter are different kinds of evidence.

## 2. 1 Enoch 6–16 — what changes across witnesses

| Passage | Witness result | Consequence for interpretation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 6:3–8 | In 4Q201 frg. 3 ii (Milik 1 iii), the OCP text preserves substantial pieces of the name Šemiḥazah, the ordinal leader list, mutual curse and Jared. At 6:7 it prints Asael as the tenth in 4Q201. Akhmim and Syncellus give materially different names and order, e.g. Akhmim Aseal well down its list, Syncellus Azalzel tenth. In OCP 4Q204's surviving list shows differences of order and an extra-looking sequence around the ninth to eleventh places. | Shemihazah/Semiazas is a reasonable cross-language identification in narrative context. Identifying every name with a unique cross-witness individual is unresolved. The number 200 is clear in the Syncellus insertion and the selected Geʿez edition. Its location in a reconstructed Aramaic line does not make it legible there. |
| 7:1–2 | 4Q201 frg. 3 ii.15–17 (Drawnel numbering, via Marro pp. 60–61) contains teaching, conception, part of a birth verb and a subsequent birth phrase. At 7:2, 4Q202 contributes a broken trace associated with birth/offspring. No stature numeral is printed as surviving in these traces. Akhmim explicitly has 3,000 cubits, as does the checked OCP English rendering of Rylands p. Syncellus gives giants, Nephilim and Elioud in a three-generation succession in place of the height. | Keep CLAIM-051 as an attestation of Charles. No Aramaic numeral was verified, including any proposed 300. The duration, size and number of offspring remain distinct questions. Line length can constrain a restoration without providing the missing reading. |
| 7:3–6 | 4Q201 frg. 3 ii.18–22 and 4Q202 preserve pieces of eating, human labor, violence against humans/animals and drinking blood. Akhmim gives an escalation from consuming human labor to consuming people, with a difficult flesh-eating clause in 7:5. | Exploitation is supported across versions. Whether the last flesh-eating clause specifically means cannibalism among giants depends on the witness and referent. The wider story addresses the limits of human provisioning. |
| 8:1–3 | 4Q202 1 ii (Milik numbering), OCP 1692–1698, gives partial Asael, iron swords and traces concerning bronze, gold, silver bracelets, stibium and eye paint. Greek 8:1 has Azael. Rylands p's OCP translation uses Azazel at 8:1 and 10:8, Asael at 13:1. OCP 4Q201 8:3 gives Shemihazah with magical instruction and Shamshiel with sun omens. Akhmim and Syncellus also name Shemihazah at 8:3. The Charles/Rylands list instead places Amazarak at the start of 8:3. | Arms and adornment are well grounded, but items, recipients, and attribution must be cited by witness. | 9:1–11 | Akhmim's narrative includes an appeal about bloodshed, Asael's instruction and Shemihazah's authority. The names at 9:1 vary. The messenger's name and the lexical descriptions of offspring are edition-specific. |
| 12–14 | Akhmim and Rylands witness Enoch's mediation. The vision's architecture belongs to the revelation's narrative. |
| 15:8–16:1 | Akhmim and Rylands distinguish the giant offspring's spirits from the heavenly fathers and make the earth their sphere. Akhmim 15:11 has a troublesome depiction including fasting and thirsting in the OCP translation. OCP Rylands depicts neither eating nor thirsting. 16:1 connects destructive activity to slaughter/death and to the horizon of great judgment. Syncellus has a parallel segment per Drawnel; Aramaic evidence for these exact clauses was not verified. | The origin and activity of the spirits are more secure at the level of the two checked versions than their precise bodily experience. 19:1 has its own language about angels and spirits; its referent should be discussed on its own. |

**Literary question.** The Shemihazah and Asael complexes have different narrative emphases. The extant sequence assigns knowledge transmission to multiple Watchers, places both accusations together in 9:6–8, and distributes sentences in 10. Reed's analysis (SRC-026) treats the problem as transmission of knowledge and redaction; Bhayro's abstract (SRC-023) favors Panopolitanus in his textual reconstruction and discusses the combined narrative. Recovering hypothetical earlier units requires literary argument and a fuller apparatus. [Cambridge chapter](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/heavenly-realms-and-earthly-realities-in-late-antique-religions/heavenly-ascent-angelic-descent-and-the-transmission-of-knowledge-in-1-enoch-616/730391A01ADC0508298D9C557B05D8D1) · [UCL thesis](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10057768/).

## 3. 1 Enoch 17–36 — close reading

Text below follows the Rylands p presentation with checks against Akhmim where it survives. “Journeys” describes Enoch's narration, 19:3 calls what he saw a vision, and the guides explain the scenes. It can present meaningful directions, landscape and celestial mechanisms within a revelation. Three overlapping registers are particularly visible: travel past mountains and waters, angelic instruction about cosmic order, and judicial or cultic scenes whose force comes from the guide's explanation.

| Passage | Description and function | Comparison and limits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 17:1–8 | Fiery beings appear human at will. Enoch visits storm and a heaven-reaching mountain, stores of thunder/lightning, the water of life, western sunset fire, and a fiery river entering a western sea. The episode ends at the river mouths and abyss. | The representation includes weapons of fire among meteorological phenomena and western sunset imagery. Compare the river-source formulas in the Ugaritic Baal Cycle via Abate pp. 27–29 and Milik's interpretation; proposed literary relationship requires a separate primary comparison. |
| 18:1–16 | Wind stores and winds supporting earth/heaven and turning celestial bodies. Seven precious-stone mountains include a throne-like central peak. A barren site past the cosmic supports holds seven burning, mountain-like stars punished for breaking their schedule. | There is compositional repetition with 21 and 24. Directional and sentencing details should stay attached to their individual passages. |
| 19:1–3 | Uriel interprets the fate of angels who mingled with women, associates their spirits with misleading humans into sacrifice to demons, and the text assigns a fate to the women. Enoch calls his experience a vision no other human has seen as he has. | At 19:2 Akhmim explicitly has sirens. Rylands OCP English also renders sirens, with the Geʿez lexeme/recensional history to be checked. The women are characterized differently in the two OCP translations (wives of transgressing angels vs women who led the angels astray). Demonic worship is the passage's polemical depiction. |
| 20:1–7 | Roster of holy angels and jurisdictions, including Raphael over human spirits, Raguel's concern with world/luminaries and Gabriel over paradise, serpents and cherubim. | The closing Greek rubric speaks of seven, yet the displayed Akhmim and Rylands p entries name six. Remiel at 20:8 in other presentations requires its own textual provenance. The holy angels here are distinguished by function from the transgressors. |
| 21:1–10 | Uriel interprets a desolate prison of seven stars. A subsequent, more terrible location has fire descending into the abyss and is identified as the prison of the angels. | Within the narrated sequence the two scenes are distinct. 18:14–16 already applies prison imagery to the stars/host. Relation among the sites and the condemned figures is an interpretive problem. |
| 22:1–14 | A mountain of rock in the west contains hollows for the dead pending judgment. Abel's spirit brings a complaint about Cain. Raphael explains a spring for the righteous and differentiated conditions for sinners and victims. | Akhmim and p both present four at 22:2 and three at 22:9. Akhmim 22:2 expressly contrasts three dark and one bright with a spring, where p's 22:2 describes dark-looking hollows and introduces the spring in the explanation. 4Q206 has a partial overlap near Abel's episode. The relation of compartments, divisions and resident groups requires interpretation. |
| 23:1–4 | At the western end of the earth Enoch sees an unceasing running fire. Raguel relates it to the heavenly luminaries and their course. | An angel's explanation supplies the cosmological reading inside the text. |
| 24:1–25:7 | Seven mountains and a fragrant imperishable tree with date-like fruit. Michael interprets the high mountain as God's throne for his visitation. Access to the tree is barred until judgment; its fruit gives life to the chosen and the tree is to be transplanted near the Lord's house. | Compare 18's seven mountains and Genesis 2–3's life-giving tree. The chapter 24–25 tree and chapter 32 wisdom tree have different scenes, properties and roles. Life and relief from sorrow, toil and plague are future promises within the account. |
| 26:1–27:5 | A watered, blessed middle of the earth with a holy mountain, neighboring elevations, ravines and a cursed valley. The guide says those who speak against God's glory will gather there for judgment. | Jerusalem, adjacent valleys and Gehenna are proposed identifications. The verses supply landscape cues and the judicial purpose. Verify named identifications against the commentary and ancient geography before equating the two. |
| 28:1–31:3 | Eastward wilderness has trees, water and dew. Successive mountains and ravines contain aromatic trees and substances. | Frankincense, myrrh, cinnamon, aloes and other identifications depend on edition and botany. Milik's aromatic-country fragments are primary points for comparing the lexicon and directions, with restoration boundaries. |
| 32:1–6 | Nard, aromatic trees, a sea and the angel Zotiel precede the Garden of Righteousness. A fragrant, grape-cluster-bearing tree of wisdom recalls the original parents' acquisition of knowledge, nakedness and expulsion. | Akhmim 32:6 breaks off after the father ate. The fuller account here follows Geʿez. Aramaic 4Q206 frg. 5 supplies independent traces of the mother and nakedness immediately beyond Akhmim's endpoint. Compare Genesis 2:9 and 3:6–24, where both trees belong to the garden scene. The Enochic itinerary gives the life-giving tree a separate setting and future. |
| 33:1–4 | Unusual beasts and birds at the edge of earth, where heaven rests. Enoch records stellar outlets, names, order, stations and times. Uriel shows and writes this knowledge. | This is a second documentary role for Enoch beside the written petition, with a heavenly teacher. Compare separately with the Astronomical Book and Jubilees 4. |
| 34:1–36:4 | Northern portals issue benign or violent weather, and western and southern portals are enumerated. Small eastern portals let stars move westward along assigned routes. Enoch ends in praise so angels and human souls may recognize the Creator's works. | Beyond Akhmim's endpoint, Geʿez gives the continuous sequence, with Aramaic fragments assigned to 33–34 and 35–36. The directional pattern is cosmological and liturgical as well as topographical. |

**Comparative scholarship.** Adam Abate, *The Domains and Procedures of the Dead*, chs. 2–3 (SRC-044), provides a Greek 22 transcription/translation and argues for reading the western mountain in a Mesopotamian and biblical frame and the dead victims' appeals in judicial terms. He discusses the alternative route through Greek underworld parallels and the uncertainty of direction of influence. A western mountain, sunset, and judicial petition are comparisons to test individually. No dependence on a particular itinerary follows from motif resemblance. Compare his pp. 21–26, 27–30, 30–44 with the actual Greek 22 and check primary comparanda at the next pass. [OhioLINK thesis](https://etd.ohiolink.edu/acprod/odb_etd/ws/send_file/send?accession=osu1721150632906397&disposition=inline).

**Human evidence across the two blocks.** People provide the labor the giants consume and bear the injuries of violence in 7 and 9. The teaching accounts picture human acquisition of practical and ritual skills and give the wives a role as recipients (7:1; 8:3). Enoch writes a petition for wrongdoers in 13 and records the order of stars in 33. In 22 Abel and other murdered people carry claims past death. The righteous future of 25:6 responds to ordinary suffering through long life and relief from toil and disease.
