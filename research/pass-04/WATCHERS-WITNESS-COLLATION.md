# Book of the Watchers — Aramaic birth lines, teachings, and the Greek and Syriac excerpts

Checked 2026-11-10. Dive 002, AF-01. Continues the [Pass 3 Watchers dossier](../pass-03/BOOK-OF-WATCHERS.md). Source and edition profiles are in the [Pass 4 catalogue](../../sources/PASS-04-CATALOGUE.md). The [claim entries](CLAIMS.md) distinguish direct readings from editorial and historical interpretation.

The underlying Watchers composition is Second Temple Aramaic, commonly dated to the third or early second century BCE, with debated dates and relationships for its constituent narratives. The dates below belong to individual copies or acts of transmission. “Ink” means surviving letters as read in a specified edition. A bracketed restoration may be plausible and still supply every letter of a word. A modern English rendering can obscure that distinction.

The frequently repeated 300-cubit figure needs a traceable witness or a named conjecture. None of these particular manuscript lines or presentations verifies it. Milik pp. 57–58 makes a separate comparison with the cedar-height imagery of Damascus Document II.18 and illustrates it by reducing 3,000 to 30 cubits. That discussion gives no 300-cubit reading and his edited restorations still use 3,000. The provenance of the repeated 300-cubit figure remains open. This result refines [CLAIM-086](../pass-03/CLAIMS-WATCHERS.md#claim-086--the-checked-aramaic-birth-traces-preserve-no-stature-numeral) by adding a plate check and the limits of two Aramaic reconstructions.

Humans occupy several roles within the narrative. They produce things with the skills they receive (Syncellus 8:1), lose the fruits of their labour (Akhmim 7:3 and the Aramaic parallels checked in Pass 3), and suffer violence (Akhmim 7:4 and Syncellus 8:3–4). In Syncellus 9:10, the dead themselves cry for relief. These clauses support observations about households, skills and dependence on human production. The exact object of eating in Akhmim 7:5 remains open to interpretation.

## 3. Syncellus excerpts, judgment and the spirits

Black's Greek text was consulted in an online transcription that identifies its print edition. Bhayro pp. 89 and 198–249 cross-reference Dindorf I pp. 20–23 and 42–46 for the two overlapping excerpts, conventionally 6:1–9:4 and 8:4–10:14. The overlap at 8:4 and 9:1–4 has distinct formulations. At 8:4 one form has survivors seek to bring their remembrance before the Lord, while another explicitly has humans cry out and ask that their case be brought in. The nearby transition into 9:1 includes different renderings of the angel names. At 10:1, Syncellus's second excerpt names Uriel for the mission to Lamech's son, where Akhmim in OCP has Istrael. Count and names belong to a particular excerpt and edition. For the separate late passage, see the Syncellus segment printed by Black at 15:8–16:1. This pass checked the Greek transcription. Black's printed apparatus and Dindorf's pages were identified through Bhayro and are pending direct inspection.

In Syncellus 9:6–10, the accusation against Azael's teaching stands alongside the authority given to Semiazas and the harm that followed the unions. In 10:4–8 Raphael is to bind Azael in darkness in Dudael and heal the injured earth. In 10:9–10 Gabriel is directed against the giants and to set them against one another. In 10:11–14 Michael is directed to bind Semiazas and the others, who see the death of their sons before confinement. These are separate orders in the text. Milik 4Q202 1 iv.5–12, pp. 172, 346, preserves pieces assigned to the latter two orders, including language about the death of the sons, a damaged seventy-generations phrase, and the great day. His continuous wording includes substantial supplements. The juxtaposition supports a literary distinction in emphasis. It does not establish the order in which earlier versions were written or joined. Bhayro's and Reed's proposals belong in that separate discussion (SRC-023, SRC-026).

Akhmim 15:8–12, Syncellus 15:8–12 and OCP Rylands put the spirits of the giants on earth and describe their harmful activity. At 15:11 both checked Greek forms say they eat nothing and thirst. The OCP Rylands rendering says they neither eat nor thirst. Syncellus 16:1 ties the spirits' destructive activity to the slaughter and the future judgment. Its wording moves between soul and flesh and applies “giants” and “Nephilim” together in this passage, unlike the sequence of begetting in its first excerpt. This makes it unsafe to extract a fixed set of biological categories from the chronographer's excerpts. No Aramaic ink was checked for 15:8–16:1, and the Geʿez comparison remains limited to the named Rylands presentation.

## 4. Michael the Syrian, Chronicle I.4

Chabot's French translation, vol. I pp. 7–8, and the Syriac hand-copy reproduced from his edition in Bhayro plate 2, p. 356, were examined. The heading attributes the marginal excerpt to Enoch. It relates the increase of people and birth of daughters, the angels' deliberation, Semyazos's concern, the oath, and the descent of two hundred in Jared's time. Chabot prints twenty leaders with a note citing the Greek for that reading. The continuation describes fourth-ranked Kokabel teaching human beings about solar movements and a scheme of 360 signs in Chabot's rendering. The number belongs to the astronomical teaching. Drawnel SRC-034 §2.7 assigns SOAA 0250(S), dated 1598, f. 5r margin ll. 42–69 to 6:1–6a and 8, with f. 5v margin ll. 1–6 compared to 8:3. Bhayro describes the core extract as 6:1–7. These scopes use different alignments for the abbreviated roster and appended teaching, so cite the actual folio or Chabot page when the distinction matters.

Michael's main narrative elsewhere in I.3–4, discussed by Bhayro pp. 86–88, supplies the daughters of Cain, the rule of Semyazos, and the spread of violence among Seth's descendants. Cite these as later reception and keep the chronicle's surrounding text distinct from its marginal Enoch quotation. The Syriac resemblance to Syncellus in places is compatible with a shared intermediary. Brock and Bhayro discuss chronographic transmission routes, including possible Alexandrian and Syriac intermediaries. The wording alone cannot settle the genealogy of the copies. Bhayro's plate 3, p. 357, was also inspected. It represents the derivative Arabic text in Syriac script, reported from British Library Or. 4402 (1846), and is a separate reception witness. A fresh collation of the underlying Syriac and Karshuni manuscripts and a reading of Brock's complete article are outstanding. The excerpt adds no direct evidence about the giants' height.
